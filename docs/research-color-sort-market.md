# Research: the color sort market, and what it means for our level goal and world map

Researched 2026-11-25 for two open decisions: the level goal (T-028: heat, clock, or limited gems) and the world map (T-026: places with 6 levels and a boss). Every claim has a link. Store pages, company statements, earnings calls and platform documents are treated as primary. Figures from analytics companies (AppMagic, Sensor Tower, AppsFlyer, Appodeal, Tenjin, Appfigures, GameAnalytics, Mistplay) are labeled **vendor data**: they are estimates, usually of in app purchase (IAP) revenue only, and they do not include ad revenue unless stated. Analysis sites and press (Deconstructor of Fun, Naavik, Gamigion, PocketGamer.biz, Mobidictum, AppSamurai) are labeled **secondary**. Anything I could not trace to a source is labeled **unverified**.

Our game, for reference: a **real time reflex sort**. Red and blue gems fall from chutes in a 3D alley and the player swipes each one left or right into the matching chest. The hits below are mostly **turn based**: nothing happens until you tap, and you can stop and think as long as you like.

### Are the hits turn based or real time?

- **Until mid 2025, nearly all of them were turn based.** Water sort, Magic Sort, Hexa Sort, screw games, Color Block Jam and Arrow Puzzle all wait for your tap. Color Block Jam is the main exception: a turn based board with a clock on top.
- **Since late 2025 the biggest growth is conveyor games with real time pressure.** Pixel Flow is the largest hit in the whole subgenre, and 5 of AppMagic's Q3 2026 top 10 (Pixel Flow, Yarn Loop, Colony Flow, Marble Sort, Cube Land) use a moving conveyor or loop. AppMagic counted 137 new releases using "pixel" and 194 using "flow" in their names or descriptions in the first half of 2026. Deconstructor of Fun describes Pixel Flow players as "actively managing space, timing, and pressure the whole time" and says skilled players can chain taps fast ("slinging") to get out of trouble ([Deconstructor of Fun, Feb 2026](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2026/2/13/pixel-flow-the-publishers-dream), secondary).
- **But even in those games the pressure comes from space, not reaction speed.** You fail Pixel Flow when the 5 slots are full, Marble Sort when the belt jams, Screwdom when the dock fills. Nothing falls past you if you are slow. The levels are fixed: Pixel Flow's boards are "fully deterministic" with no randomness (same Deconstructor of Fun article).

### How have pure reflex sort games done?

I found **no commercial evidence of a successful reflex sort game** of our type (items fall, you swipe each one to the correct side). The examples I found are small: Flick Sort (swipe falling cards left or right by a changing rule) has too few ratings for the App Store to show an average ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/flick-sort/id6752952597)); Sortify and Mad Swipe are itch.io or early access releases. None publish revenue or downloads. Absence of evidence is not proof of failure, but no reflex sort game appears in any top 10 or subgenre report above.

The closest large category is hypercasual, where most reflex games lived. Its retention is clearly worse than puzzle (section 4), and Voodoo, the biggest hypercasual publisher, says the old model of constantly launching new games "to offset steep declines of up to -40% per year now belongs to the past" ([Voodoo, 2026](https://voodoo.io/news/2025-a-year-of-growth-and-transformation-for-voodoo), primary).

### What the numbers mean for us

- Sort is real money but a small slice: a few hundred million dollars a year across hundreds of games, with one or two winners taking much of each subgenre (Color Block Jam about 75% of block, Screwdom about 45% of screw, Magic Sort about 18% of sort, all per Naavik).
- The newest winners added **real time pressure through a conveyor** while keeping the player in charge of when to act. That is the closest proven relative of our game. Our game goes one step further: the gems arrive on the game's schedule, not the player's.
- No game in the charts asks the player to react to items as fast as they fall. That is the unproven part of our design.

## 2. Levels

### How levels are won and lost

| Fail rule | Games | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| No fail at all | Water Sort Puzzle | "Restart freely if you get stuck" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/1514542157)). Undo is limited and refilled by ads (store reviews) |
| Run out of space or slots | Pixel Flow (5 slots), Screwdom and Screw Jam (dock, "often just five" holders per Naavik), Marble Sort (belt), Hexa Sort (board) | The most common rule among 2025 and 2026 hits. Naavik notes that filling the last slot brings up an out of moves screen with a paid continue |
| No moves left | Magic Sort | Store reviews complain hidden colors make one wrong pour fatal ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/magic-sort/id6499209744)) |
| Countdown timer | Color Block Jam, Match Factory, Triple Match 3D | Color Block Jam: "plan your moves before running out of time" (store description). Reviewers report some levels around one minute. Naavik says its "strict time limits" push players to buy extra time |
| Mistake budget | Arrow Puzzle (a set number of errors per level; the source says two, then one) | Closest to our heat and limited gems ideas |
| Move limit | Hexa Away | Gamigion called moves instead of time "an interesting choice" and suggested it "didn't really" work ([Gamigion](https://www.gamigion.com/hexa-away-scaled-to-130k-a-day-then-declined-heavily/), secondary) |

Almost every game that can fail uses **lives**. Color Block Jam's FAQ: you lose a life when time runs out; with no lives left you wait, spend coins to refill up to 5, or watch a rewarded ad for one life ([Rollic FAQ](https://rollic.helpshift.com/hc/en/24-color-block-jam/faq/1222-how-do-i-play-color-block-jam), primary). Deconstructor of Fun says hybrid casual puzzles "adopt a 'limited lives' system similar to that seen in most casual puzzles," offset by "end game offers that encourage players to keep going" ([link](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/2/3/hybridcasual-puzzles-expanding-the-puzzle-market), secondary).

### Level length and the difficulty curve

The best data I found is Gamigion's timed playthrough of **Pixel Flow levels 1 to 300** ([Gamigion, 2026-02-03](https://gamigion.substack.com/p/pixel-flow-1-300), secondary; the full spreadsheet was not accessible):

| Levels | Normal level | Hard level | Very hard level | Share of levels that are hard or very hard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 100 | 71 s | 143 s | 180 s | 26 of 100 |
| 101 to 200 | 147 s | 239 s | 319 s | 31 of 100 |
| 201 to 300 | 172 s | 249 s | 302 s | 27 of 100 |

So in the biggest hit, early levels take about a minute, later ones two and a half to three minutes, and **roughly one level in four is marked hard or very hard**. Playing all 300 without a loss took 13 hours 22 minutes.

Other points:

- Arrow Puzzle levels take about 30 to 90 seconds ([Gamigion](https://www.gamigion.com/?p=25286), secondary).
- Screwdom "feels relaxed for the first 3 to 4 minutes" of a level ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/the-screw-puzzle-gold-rush), secondary).
- The pattern is a "roller coaster": "Levels gradually increase in challenge until a 'hard' or 'super hard' level appears," then ease off ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/2/3/hybridcasual-puzzles-expanding-the-puzzle-market), secondary). Pixel Flow, Magic Sort and Color Block Jam all label hard levels on screen (store reviews and Gamigion).
- Too many hard levels in a row is the top complaint in Magic Sort's App Store reviews ("hard levels followed by more hard levels").
- Fixed, repeatable levels matter. Pixel Flow is fully deterministic. An industry summary credits Color Block Jam's engagement to "a sharp difficulty curve and a fairness perception (deterministic puzzle levels) that encourages retries" ([source](https://writingmate.github.io/zeptolab-game-deepresearch/reports/hybrid-casual-market-research), **unverified**: an AI generated report).
- Color Block Jam's own FAQ: "every level is designed to be beatable" even when hard (FAQ page, now returning 404; quoted via search result, **unverified**).

### How levels are grouped

- **Most sort hits have little or no map or decorating layer.** The sort, screw and Pixel Flow write ups describe a level counter, hard level labels and live events, not a map. Color Block Jam is described as having "only basic events" (industry summary, unverified). AppMagic ties Pixel Flow's success to "a consistent event schedule."
- **They lean on live events and collections instead.** Take-Two credits Match Factory's "collectible album" (2026-05-21 call). Color Block Jam runs time limited competitions (three of its five active events, per Naavik). Pixel Flow reviews mention a "Fire Quest" tournament and lava challenges. Marble Sort runs a Streak Bonus and a "Sugar Rush" event (AppMagic).
- **The bigger casual puzzles do use a map with a meta.** Royal Match and Candy Crush (covered in `research-retention-and-pacing.md`) group levels into areas or episodes of 10 to over 100 levels. Magic Sort's description mentions a cauldron and "Mystical Ingredients" as a light collection layer.
- **Boss levels:** I found no sort game with a named boss level. Fan guides call Color Block Jam level 80 "boss style" (secondary). The genre's equivalent is the labeled "super hard" level.

### What the numbers mean for us

- Short levels win: about 1 minute early, rising to 2 to 3 minutes. A 220 second level (prototype B) is the length of a late game Pixel Flow *hard* level. Early levels should be closer to 60 to 90 seconds.
- Plan about one hard level in four to seven, labeled as hard before it starts, and never two in a row early on.
- Fixed levels (the same gem sequence every attempt) are the norm among the hits. A random sequence makes a fail feel like bad luck.

### Ad formats

- **Interstitials** "after every level or every second level" are typical ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/2/3/hybridcasual-puzzles-expanding-the-puzzle-market), secondary). Pixel Flow shows interstitials on failure and optionally after wins, turned up after about level 20. Screwdom shows one after every level.
- **Rewarded ads**: an extra life (Color Block Jam, Pixel Flow), doubling end of level coins (Screw Jam, Block Jam 3D, Hexa Sort, Color Block Jam), undo (Water Sort). Pixel Flow deliberately offers no rewarded ad that skips difficulty.
- **Banners**: not mentioned for any top sort game. Appodeal says banners "consistently have the lowest eCPM across all regions" (below).

### What they sell (App Store US, read 2026-11-25)

| Game | Fail offer | Remove ads | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| Color Block Jam | "Fail Offer" $4.99 | "No Ads Bundle" $11.99 | Coin packs $1.99 to $29.99, bundles to $29.99, "Golden Ticket" pass $9.99. Coins buy boosters and **extra time** (Rollic FAQ) |
| Pixel Flow! | "Fail Offer 1" $5.99 | "No Ads" $7.99 | Gold $1.99 to $29.99, bundles to $34.99, "Golden Pass" $9.99 |
| Magic Sort! | none listed | none listed | Coins $1.99 to $14.99, bundles to $19.99, "Endless Portal" $5.99 |
| Water Sort Puzzle | "Revival Pack" $2.99 | "No Ads" $2.99 | Gems $1.99 to $99.99, "Premium" $4.99 |

Inside one game the split can be lopsided: in Marble Sort the main "Marble Sort Pack" was 85.5% of IAP revenue, No Ads offers 3.7%, and fail offers only 1.9% ([AppMagic, 2026-06-15](https://appmagic.rocks/blog/voodoo-new-big-three), vendor data). Naavik describes the screw game version as the out of moves screen with a paid continue, and Deconstructor of Fun calls Pixel Flow's key purchase "paying to continue after a failure instead of replaying" ([link](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/pixel-flow-and-the-rise-of-sort-puzzles), secondary).

### What the numbers mean for us

- The genre's money comes from the failed level: lives, a paid continue that adds room (a slot, time, moves), and boosters bought with coins. Whatever our level goal is, it needs a clear "you were close, add a little more" moment.
- Plan both a No Ads purchase ($3 to $12 in these games) and rewarded ads for a life and for doubling coins. Skip banners.
- Every top title has a coin store and a pass. A pure ads game is viable (Arrow Puzzle) but needs far more players (section 5).

### Sessions and spending

- Pixel Flow: "close to one hour per day across roughly ten sessions" for engaged players at scale, while early retention is "relatively modest for the category" ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2026/2/13/pixel-flow-the-publishers-dream), secondary).
- Puzzle leads all genres in players who pay by Day 30 (7.1% Android, 7.8% iOS) and in speed to first purchase (1.6 to 1.7 days). US only, AppsFlyer metrics for 2023 and 2024 ([Mistplay and AppsFlyer via GameDev Reports, 2025-05-27](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/mistplay-and-appsflyer-player-loyalty), vendor data, secondary). - **Not found:** session length and sessions per day for puzzle or sort specifically. GameAnalytics publishes genre numbers only as charts, and Mistplay's session table names strategy and match as leaders without puzzle values.

### What the numbers mean for us

- If we land as a puzzle game, a realistic target is about 30 to 35% Day 1, 11 to 14% Day 7 and 5 to 8% Day 30. If players treat us like hypercasual, expect about 23 to 28% Day 1 and 5 to 6% Day 7. That gap is the single biggest money risk (section 5).
- Engaged sort players play many short sessions (about ten a day in Pixel Flow), so a 1 to 3 minute level fits.

### Store fees on purchases (primary)

- Apple: 15% on in app purchases for developers under $1M a year ([Apple Small Business Program](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/)).

- Ad revenue has no store fee; eCPM is already what the publisher is paid.

### Worked estimate: what $30k a month takes

Assumptions, all stated:

1. **$30,000 a month is net to Anthony**, so about **$1,000 a day**. For IAP, the per player figure is already after store fees.
2. **Three revenue levels per daily player:** $0.06 (ads only, Arrow Puzzle level), $0.11 (ads plus modest purchases: $0.06 ads plus about $0.05 net IAP), and $0.25 (purchase led, still less than half of Pixel Flow's $0.55).
3. **Daily players per daily install** comes from summing a retention curve fitted through Day 1 and Day 7 (a power curve, cut off at 180 days). For puzzle level retention (29% Day 1, 11% Day 7, the AppsFlyer US Android figures) each install produces about **8.5 days of play**. For the median game in GameAnalytics (22% and 4%) it is about **2.7 days**. Hypercasual US Android (23% and 5%) gives about 3.4.
4. Steady state: after several months, daily players stay level because new installs replace those who leave. Reaching it takes months of steady installs.
5. Ignores income tax, ad network payment delays, and seasonality (Q4 ad prices are the highest of the year).

| Revenue per daily player | Daily players needed | Installs needed with puzzle retention | Installs needed with median retention | Value of one install over 180 days (puzzle retention) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0.06 (ads only) | about 16,700 | about 2,000 a day, 59,000 a month | about 6,200 a day, 185,000 a month | about $0.51 |
| $0.11 (ads plus modest IAP) | about 9,100 | about 1,100 a day, 32,000 a month | about 3,400 a day, 101,000 a month | about $0.94 |
| $0.25 (purchase led) | about 4,000 | about 470 a day, 14,000 a month | about 1,500 a day, 44,000 a month | about $2.12 |

How to read it:

- **Retention matters more than ad placement.** Moving from median to puzzle level retention cuts the installs needed by about two thirds at every revenue level.
- **Paid US installs do not pay back** at these levels: one US puzzle install costs $4.81 to $9.48, against an install value of $0.51 to $2.12. Profitable paid growth would need cheap countries (Brazil Android puzzle $0.49) plus a game that earns well there, or a far stronger purchase side.
- So for a solo developer, $30k a month realistically means **about 10,000 daily players**, fed by **roughly 1,000 to 2,000 mostly free installs a day** (store featuring, search, social video, cross promotion), with puzzle level retention. Spark & Spiral's "well over $10k a month" from about 30,000 monthly players with ads alone is a real data point a third of the way there.

### What the numbers mean for us

- $30k a month is plausible but sits well above the median outcome; most games never reach 10,000 daily players. Hitting it depends first on Day 7 retention near 11 to 14%, and second on a purchase side (fail offer, coins, pass, No Ads) on top of ads.
- Do not plan on buying US installs until the game earns at least $2 to $5 per install.

## 6. What it means for us

### T-028: which level goal the evidence favors

**C, limited gems (178 fall, 150 must be sorted), matches the genre best.** Reasons:

1. **It is the genre's dominant fail rule in another form.** The 2025 and 2026 hits fail you when a limited resource runs out: Pixel Flow's 5 slots, the screw games' dock, Marble Sort's belt, Arrow Puzzle's small mistake budget. "28 gems you can afford to lose" is the same idea, and it is easy to read on screen as a progress bar (sorted out of 150) and a remaining count.
2. **It gives the clearest fail offer.** The genre's money comes from the moment you just miss: "you sorted 146 of 150, add 10 gems for $X or an ad" is the same shape as Pixel Flow's and Color Block Jam's fail offers ($5.99 and $4.99). It also produces the near miss moment that, per the existing research doc, most strongly drives "one more try."
3. **The length is fixed and predictable.** A level always ends when the last gem lands, so we can tune it to 60 to 90 seconds early and 2 to 3 minutes later, as Pixel Flow does.

**B, the clock, has precedent but fits us worse.** Color Block Jam and Match Factory prove a timer can work in a big hit, and Color Block Jam sells extra time. But in those games the timer is the *only* source of time pressure on a board that otherwise waits for you. Our gems already fall on their own schedule, so a clock adds a second pressure on top of the reflex one. The 3 second penalty per lost gem also makes the clock a mistake budget in disguise, which is C with an extra number to read. Weak extra evidence: the timed subgenre (block, mostly Color Block Jam) has the lowest Day 30 of the three (about 4% against 8% for sort and 17% for screw, Naavik). That is a correlation, not proof that timers cause it.

**A, heat, has no direct match among the hits.** The closest is Arrow Puzzle's mistake budget, which is a fixed count, not a meter that rises and falls. A meter that can cool down is harder to read and harder to price as a continue. If heat stays (it is part of the heist theme), it fits better as a pressure inside a level (heat speeds up chutes or raises traps) than as the win or fail rule.

Two changes the evidence supports whichever goal wins:

- **Use a fixed gem sequence per level.** Every top hit is deterministic, so a failed level feels like the player's mistake and a retry feels fair. Random falls make a near miss feel like luck.
- **Cap early levels at about 60 to 90 seconds.** The current 220 second prototype length matches a late Pixel Flow hard level, not an early one.

### T-026: the world map and boss levels

- **The structure fits the genre's difficulty rhythm.** Six levels then a boss gives one hard level every seventh. Pixel Flow runs about one hard or very hard level in four, so we could also mark one of the six (say the fourth) as hard, and treat the boss as the "super hard" level the genre already uses. Label it before it starts.
- **A map is more meta than most sort hits have.** Most rely on a level counter plus events, albums and passes. The big casual puzzles (Royal Match, Candy Crush) do use maps or areas. A map with places is a reasonable middle and a point of difference, but do not expect it alone to retain players; the hits retain through short levels, a steady difficulty rhythm and a calendar of events.
- **Keep the fail cost to one attempt and one life.** That matches the whole genre (lives, regenerating, refilled by coins or a rewarded ad), and it fits T-026's "a fail costs only the attempt."
- **Clues for the boss work as the reward for winning ordinary levels.** I found no sort game with a boss or clue system, so this is untested in the genre, not contradicted by it.

### Is a real time sort a risk?

**Yes, a real one, but a narrower one than "turn based wins and real time loses."**

What reduces the risk:

- The biggest sort hit of 2025 to 2026 (Pixel Flow, about $200M in its first year) and half of the current top 10 put real time pressure on the player through a moving conveyor. Players of this genre clearly accept a game that moves while they think.
- Color Block Jam, the most successful title in Rollic's history, puts a countdown on every level.

What raises it:

- **In every hit, the player still decides when to act**, and failure comes from a planning mistake (filling the slots), not from being too slow. In our game a gem that falls while you are slow is lost. I found no reflex sort game in any chart, and the few that exist have almost no ratings.
- **Reflex games used to be hypercasual, and hypercasual retains about half as well as puzzle by Day 7** (AppsFlyer Q3 2026). At the same revenue per player, the worked estimate in section 5 needs about three times the installs at median or hypercasual retention as at puzzle retention.
- Puzzle players are the ones who pay (top genre for payers by Day 30). If our game plays like an arcade reflex test, we may get hypercasual players who mostly watch ads.

What would lower the risk, as design directions to test rather than conclusions:

- Let the player's decisions, not just reaction speed, decide the result: traps to pocket rather than sort, chests that change, gems that must be held, so a fail feels like "I chose wrong" rather than "I was too slow."
- Keep the early fall speed gentle so the first levels read as a puzzle, and introduce speed as one of the difficulty levers, the way Pixel Flow introduces new blockers over its first 300 levels.
- Watch Day 7 retention in the first soft launch. If it lands nearer 5 to 6% (hypercasual) than 11 to 14% (puzzle), that is the signal the real time core is the problem.

## TLDR

- **Leaders (vendor data):** sort, screw and block puzzles made about $0.7B in store purchases in 2025, a small but tripling slice of a $8 to $10B puzzle market. The top titles in Q3 2026 were Pixel Flow (Loom Games, about $200M in its first year), Block Out and Magic Sort (Grand Games), Screwdom (iKame) and Color Block Jam (Rollic, Take-Two's "highest grossing title in Rollic history").
- **Turn based or real time:** the classic hits are turn based. The newest and biggest add real time pressure through a moving conveyor, and Color Block Jam adds a clock, but in every hit the player chooses when to act and fails by running out of space, not by being slow. I found no successful reflex sort game like ours.
- **Levels:** fail by running out of slots, moves or time; water sort has no fail. Lives everywhere. Pixel Flow levels take about 70 seconds early and 2.5 to 3 minutes later, with about one level in four marked hard. Levels are fixed, not random. Most sort hits use events and albums rather than a map.
- **Money:** IAP led (Rollic, Magic Sort, Pixel Flow at 60 to 70% IAP), with interstitials after levels, rewarded ads for lives and doubled coins, a No Ads purchase ($3 to $12), and a paid fail offer ($5 to $6).
- **Retention (vendor data):** US puzzle installs from ads keep 29 to 34% on Day 1 and 11 to 14% on Day 7, about twice hypercasual's Day 7. Day 30 is about 8% for sort, 17% for screw, 4% for the timed block subgenre.
- **$30k a month:** about $1,000 a day, which is roughly 10,000 daily players at $0.06 to $0.25 per player per day, fed by about 1,000 to 2,000 mostly free installs a day if retention is puzzle level, and three times that if it is not. US paid installs ($4.81 to $9.48 for puzzle) do not pay back at these revenue levels.
- **T-028:** the evidence favors **C, limited gems**: it is the genre's own "limited room" fail rule, it gives the clearest paid continue, and it fixes level length. Use a fixed gem sequence per level and cut early levels to 60 to 90 seconds. A clock has precedent but doubles up the time pressure our falling gems already create; heat has no match among the hits.
- **T-026:** six levels plus a boss matches the genre's hard level rhythm (add one hard level mid place). A map is more meta than most sort hits have, which is fine, but events will matter as much.
- **Real time risk:** real. The proven version of real time pressure leaves the player in charge of timing. Make decisions, not just reaction speed, decide a level, keep early speed gentle, and treat a soft launch Day 7 near 5% as the warning sign.
